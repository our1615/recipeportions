Understanding Ingredient Density: Why 1 Cup of Flour Isn't Always 120g

By Olivia Hartmann June 17, 2026 7 min read Concepts

I used to think baking was about following instructions. Then I realized it's about understanding what those instructions actually mean. And nobody ever told me that a cup of flour could be three different things depending on how you scoop it.

My first real wake-up call was a pound cake that came out like a brick. I followed the recipe exactly. One cup of flour, one cup of sugar, one cup of butter, one cup of eggs. But I used the same measuring cup for everything. I scooped the flour by dipping the cup straight into the bag. Turns out that method gives you about 150 grams of flour per cup. The recipe assumed I was spooning and leveling, which is about 120 grams. That 30 gram difference doesn't sound like much, but in a pound cake, it's the difference between tender and tooth-breaking.

After that disaster, I started weighing everything. But then I had a new problem. Recipes written in cups didn't tell me what weight they expected. So I had to build my own density chart. Now I know that one cup of all-purpose flour can be anywhere from 100 grams to 180 grams depending on how you measure it. Sifted before measuring is about 100 grams. Spooned and leveled is 120. Dipped and swept is 150. Packed is 180. That's an 80% range. Imagine building a house where the bricks could be 80% heavier or lighter depending on how you picked them up. Your house would fall down.

My kitchen would fall down too. But I didn't know any of this for the first few years of baking. I just followed recipes and blamed myself when things went wrong. It wasn't me. It was the measuring.

Let me give you a specific example. A chocolate cake recipe calls for 1 cup of flour and 1 cup of sugar. If you dip and sweep the flour, you get 150 grams. If you spoon and level the sugar, you get 200 grams. That's a ratio of 150 to 200, or 0.75. But if you had spooned and leveled the flour, you'd have 120 grams of flour to 200 grams of sugar, a ratio of 0.6. That's a completely different cake. Same recipe, different execution.

When you scale a recipe, these differences multiply. You're not just dealing with one cup being off. You're dealing with ten cups being off by the same percentage. A 25% error at 1x is a 25% error at 10x. You'll be off by pounds, not grams.

That's why I switched to weight years ago. I haven't used a measuring cup for dry ingredients in maybe five years. Everything goes on the scale. My scale lives on the counter. If you visit my house, you'll see it next to the coffee maker. It's not a fancy tool. It cost $22. But it's the most important tool in my kitchen.

Here's something that still surprises people. Different types of flour have different densities even when measured the same way. Bread flour is heavier than all-purpose. Whole wheat is about the same as all-purpose by weight, but it behaves differently because of the bran. Almond flour is much lighter. Coconut flour is heavier and absorbs liquid like a sponge.

I keep a laminated chart inside my cabinet door with the most common densities. All-purpose flour, 120 grams per cup spooned. Bread flour, 127 grams. Whole wheat, 120 grams. Rye, 110 grams. Almond, 96 grams. Coconut, 128 grams. Granulated sugar, 200 grams. Brown sugar packed, 220 grams. Powdered sugar unsifted, 120 grams. Butter, 227 grams. Oil, 218 grams. Milk, 244 grams. Honey, 340 grams.

That chart has saved me more times than I can count. When I see a recipe online that uses cups, I don't panic. I just convert each ingredient using my chart. Then I weigh everything. My results are consistent every time.

The tricky part is when a recipe doesn't tell you how the author measured. Most don't. They just say "1 cup flour" and assume you know. I assume they used the spoon and level method because that's the standard taught in culinary schools. But not everyone went to culinary school. Some people dip. Some people shake. Some people pack without realizing it.

I've developed a habit of reading the comments on recipe blogs before I try anything. If multiple people say the cake was too dry, I suspect they dipped the flour. If they say it was too wet, maybe they sifted. The comments tell you what the author actually meant.

Scaling a recipe that uses cups is a two-step process for me. First, I convert everything to grams using my density chart. Second, I apply my scaling factor. I never scale the cup measurements directly. That's a recipe for disaster.

Let me walk you through an example. I found a muffin recipe online that said 2 cups flour, 1 cup sugar, 1/2 cup butter, 2 eggs, 1 cup milk. I want to make 4x the recipe. Instead of multiplying 2 cups by 4 to get 8 cups, I convert first. 2 cups flour at 120 grams each is 240 grams. Times 4 is 960 grams. 1 cup sugar is 200 grams, times 4 is 800 grams. 1/2 cup butter is 113.5 grams, times 4 is 454 grams. 2 eggs is about 100 grams, times 4 is 400 grams. 1 cup milk is 244 grams, times 4 is 976 grams.

Then I adjust for leavening and salt separately. See how that works? The cups disappear. They become grams. And grams are honest.

Another thing I learned about density is that it changes with temperature and humidity. Flour left out in a humid kitchen will absorb moisture and get heavier. A cup of flour in July might weigh 125 grams instead of 120. A cup in January might be 118. This is why professional bakeries control their environment. For home bakers, you just accept a small margin of error. But it's another reason to use weight. Weight doesn't change with humidity. 120 grams is 120 grams no matter what.

I had a student once who insisted on using cups because that's how her grandmother baked. I didn't argue. I just asked her to bake two batches of the same cookie recipe. One using cups, one using a scale. The cup batch varied every time. The weighed batch was identical every time. She switched to a scale after that.

If you only learn one thing from this article, learn this. Buy a scale. Use it for dry ingredients. Ignore anyone who tells you that cups are fine. They are not fine. They are the enemy of consistent scaling.

 But honestly, you don't need fancy tools for this. You just need a scale and a density chart. Write down the densities of the ingredients you use most. Keep that paper in your kitchen. After a few months, you'll memorize them.

I have a few numbers burned into my brain. 120 grams for a cup of flour. 200 for sugar. 227 for butter. 244 for milk. 230 for baking soda. 290 for table salt. I wake up in the middle of the night and these numbers are just there.

That's what happens when you bake every day for years. The math becomes background noise. You stop thinking about it. You just do it.

Now when I see a recipe that says "1 cup flour, sifted", I know they want me to sift first, then spoon and level. That gives about 100 grams. If it says "1 cup sifted flour", they want me to measure first, then sift. That gives about 120 grams that then becomes fluffy. The order of words matters. Most people don't know that.

This stuff is the difference between okay baking and great baking. It's not about talent. It's about attention. Pay attention to how you measure. Pay attention to how the recipe was written. Pay attention to the numbers.

The brick pound cake I made years ago? I still remember how disappointed I was. But I'm grateful for it. That brick taught me to question everything. Now I don't trust cups. I don't trust "a handful". I don't trust "until it looks right". I trust grams.

— Olivia

P.S. My kitchen scale is 8 years old. The button labels have worn off. I know which button is tare by feel. That's not a flex. That's just what happens when you use a tool every single day.
