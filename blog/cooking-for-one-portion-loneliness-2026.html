Cooking For One Portion Loneliness 2026

2026-07-23 · 2 min read · General
General

I cook for one. Have for three years. And my fridge is a graveyard of good intentions. Half an onion. A third of a bell pepper. Two servings of soup that I will never finish. The recipe said "serves 4." I am 1. And math is not my friend when I am hungry and tired at 7 PM.

Food waste is the silent tax on single-person households. The average single adult throws out $600 worth of food annually. Six hundred dollars. That is not carelessness. That is the mismatch between recipe design and reality. Recipes are written for families. For dinner parties. For "serves 4-6" which actually serves 8. And single people are left dividing, storing, and eventually throwing away the excess.

I tried halving recipes. It works for some things. Soups. Stews. Simple sautés. But baking? Disaster. Half an egg is not a thing. Half a teaspoon of baking powder in a full recipe is already finicky. Halve it and you are guessing. And guessing in baking is how you get flat cookies and dense cakes. I stopped baking for a year because I could not face another failed half-batch.

Then I changed my approach. I stopped fighting recipes and started planning for leftovers. But not the "eat the same thing for five days" kind. The "intentionally cook components" kind. I roast a whole chicken on Sunday. I eat the breast Monday. I shred the thigh for tacos Tuesday. I boil the carcass for soup Wednesday. One chicken. Four meals. Zero waste. And I am not bored because each meal is different.

I also embraced the freezer. Not as a last resort, but as a tool. I make a big batch of soup and freeze it in single portions. I cook rice and freeze half. I buy bread, slice it, and freeze it. The freezer is my portion control. It lets me cook for four and eat for one, without the waste. It just requires planning. And planning is free.

The loneliness is harder. Cooking for one is not just about portions. It is about the absence of someone to share with. I used to skip meals because cooking felt pointless without company. I ordered takeout because it felt like a treat, even when I could not afford it. I ate cereal for dinner because it was easier than facing an empty table with a full plate.

I fixed that too. I set my table properly, even alone. I light a candle. I play music. I eat at the table, not the couch. I make it an event. Because I deserve an event. Because nourishment is not just physical. It is ritual. It is self-respect. And it is the difference between surviving and living.

If you cook for one, stop fighting the recipes. Start planning for yourself. Use your freezer. Set your table. And remember: a meal made with care is never wasted, even if you are the only one eating it.

— Emily Hartwell, Portland, OR

Emily is a registered dietitian who learned to cook for one after divorce. She believes in good food, proper portions, and never eating cereal for dinner twice in a row.
